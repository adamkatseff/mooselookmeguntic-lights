How this is built

Positions come from public orbit data for all 10,633 Starlink satellites, calculated second by second for your geotagged spot (44.8488° N, 70.7540° W). A satellite is drawn only while it is in sunlight, above the ridge, and close to the mirror angle that bounces sunlight off its flat underside toward you.

Brightness is a rough model: the further from a perfect reflection, the dimmer the flare, and light this close to the horizon is dimmed and turned orange by about 20 times more air than overhead. Magnitude 0 is about as bright as the brightest stars.

Fainter near-miss flares adds Starlinks up to 6° from a perfect reflection and uses a gentler fade with angle, the generous end of what's plausible. All sunlit satellites shows every satellite in sunlight in this patch of sky, flaring or not: grey dots are Starlinks, blue dots are other satellites (Iridium, OneWeb, Chinese and Russian satellites, and others). Their labels give an estimated steady brightness, typically magnitude 8–11 at this distance and height. The eye can see to about magnitude 6 on a very dark night, so these were most likely invisible except when flaring.

Stars come from the Yale Bright Star Catalog (via the d3-celestial sky-chart project), shown with their true colors and placed for your exact time and spot. Stars and flares are dimmed and reddened by the same amount of air, and both are lifted by about 0.4° of atmospheric bending this close to the horizon, so you can compare them directly. The 94%-lit moon was 46° up behind you to the south-southeast, which brightened the whole sky. On a moonlit night most people see stars down to about magnitude 4 to 4.5, so that is the default for "Faintest visible". Lower numbers mean brighter.

Reimagined motion. In the upper half of the Beauty slider, the lights let go of their recorded paths. When each one appears and fades still follows the real flares, but its movement is reinterpreted as people in awe might remember it. A lone light hovers and drifts. Two lights circle each other. Three or more arrange themselves into a triangle that slowly turns, and when a fourth arrives a second triangle forms and turns the other way. Every ten seconds a gathering draws in or opens out. Their wakes trace the dance, and a light that goes out leaves a fading ember. The motion keeps living even while the model is paused. A caption on the sky marks when this is on, and the "Flaring now" table always shows the real satellites and positions.

Video twinkle makes every flare sparkle the way the lights do in your iPhone videos, at any Beauty setting. That behavior was measured frame by frame from IMG_1067, IMG_1068 and IMG_1069. The color comes in runs of a few frames (mostly cream, with red, salmon and gold, and the odd green or violet flash) as a glow around a paler core. Brightness flickers frame to frame, the shape wobbles, a red-orange fringe sits below, and tiny white specks come and go. This chromatic scintillation is real: light this close to the horizon crosses so much turbulent air that its colors scatter unevenly from moment to moment. Twinkle amount sets how strong all of it is. Close-up shows a magnified view of the brightest light in the corner of the sky.

Symbolic geometry is an artistic layer that appears in the upper half of the Beauty slider. It doesn't move any light from its calculated position. Lights that are up together are joined by living threads, with pulses of light running between them. When three or more gather, the three brightest form a softly lit triangle, and a hexagram of two counter-rotating triangles turns around the cluster's center while a heartbeat ring breathes outward. All of it is mirrored in the lake. The satellites themselves fly independent orbits; the figure is drawn from where they happen to be at each moment. Turn it off with the checkbox beside the slider.

Beauty changes only how the light is felt, never where or when anything is. Every flare, star and reflection stays at its calculated position and second. Toward Extraordinary, the night darkens to a deep indigo with faint airglow, and each light becomes a true pinpoint, as the eye sees a satellite: brighter flares grow more intense rather than larger, and their amber color only emerges as they brighten. Behind each one, a ribbon of its path cools from gold through rose to violet and lingers after the light has gone, the way an afterimage stays in the eye. The still lake doubles the whole procession, and a thin mist on the water catches the glow. Low stars flicker in color, as they really do through that much air. Labels fall away.
